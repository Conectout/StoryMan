The Grand Escapade of Barnaby's Sourdough

It was a crisp Sunday morning when Barnaby realized his artisanal sourdough starter, affectionately named "Dough-lilah," had developed an ego. He had tucked her inside the warmest spot in the house—the linen closet right above the boiler—with a light cheesecloth cover and an encouraging pep talk.

By 2:00 PM, Dough-lilah had breached containment.

When Barnaby swung open the louvered door to grab a fresh tea towel, he was met not with neatly folded Egyptian cotton, but with a silent, beige avalanche. She had digested two washcloths, draped herself lovingly over a vintage floral quilt, and was creeping across the laminate floor with the glacial menace of a well-leavened swamp monster.

Armed with only a spatula and a stainless steel salad bowl, Barnaby attempted diplomacy. "Easy now," he murmured, backing slowly toward the stove. Dough-lilah offered only a soft, bubbling *blorp* in response. By evening, the entire first floor smelled richly of warm brioche, and the neighbor’s beagle was sitting on the porch window sill, staring at the kitchen with raw existential reverence. Barnaby baked sixteen unintended baguettes that night just to clear the hallway.

By Arthur Finch

The Keeper of Forgotten Teacups

Clara operated a repair shop on the corner of Rue des Violettes where she only accepted porcelain that carried grief. People brought her porcelain cups cracked down the ribbing, chipped saucers left behind by grandmothers, and milk jugs fractured by clumsy, hurried mornings.

She did not hide the fractures. Using an ancient resin laced with real gold powder, she practiced the art of *kintsugi*—mending what was broken by honoring its scars. Every line of gold told a quiet truth: that breakage was not the end of a vessel’s dignity, but the moment its true history began.

One rainy dusk, a young man brought her a plain white cup split clean down the center. He spoke little, merely resting his palm beside it. Clara asked no questions; she simply set to work. Under the yellow lamp of her workbench, her needle-tipped brushes laid rivers of gilt along the split edges, drawing them back into communion.

When the man returned a week later, he held the mended cup up to the autumn sunlight. The gold seams caught the afternoon warmth, radiant and unashamed. For the first time in many months, he exhaled a breath he seemed to have held since summer—reminded that life, even when shattered, can hold sweetness once again.

By Elena Vane
